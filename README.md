
My interest in computer science did not begin in a classroom, it began with a charging station. During my final year of Electronics and Communication Engineering, I built an RFID-based EV charging system designed to reduce dependency on fossil fuels by authenticating and managing charging sessions through embedded hardware and code. Watching a system I had written control physical hardware, securely and reliably, was the moment I realized I wanted to spend my career building software, not just studying circuits.
Reflecting on B.E in Electronics and Communication Engineering at VTU gave me the technical base I now want to extend into computer science. Courses in digital system design, microcontrollers, embedded systems, and Verilog HDL taught me how hardware and software meet, while C programming, operating systems, and computer networks introduced the software side.  Throughout my undergraduate program, I have worked on four major projects that honed my technical,managerial, and interpersonal skills. Since then, my work as a software engineer has shown me how far a programming foundation can take me, and where my knowledge stops. I now want to combine my electronics background with formal computer science.

That realization led me to actively bridge the gap between my electronics background and computer science. I built a car information management platform during my internship, and outside my coursework, I completed certifications in Machine Learning and Data Science (Coursera), AWS (Udemy), and structured problem-solving and data structures practice (GeeksforGeeks) — deliberately filling in the software fundamentals my degree hadn't covered, one course and one project at a time.
For the past two years, I have worked as a Software Engineer at First American India, on the AttorneyFirst platform — a title insurance system supporting the closing process for homes across Chicago, connecting attorneys, examiners, and homeowners through one platform. My work here has been where theory turned into measurable impact:
	•	I trained and integrated an OCR and document-intelligence model that reduced order creation from 5–10 minutes across five manual pages to under 40 seconds. I trained the pipeline in Azure on a large document set using EC2, and designed the interface myself.
	•	I led an end-to-end test automation effort — a 10-case UI smoke suite and a 9,000-test API regression suite using Playwright, TypeScript, and Bruno — that cut our release sanity testing from a full day to about two hours.
	•	I contributed to an AWS infrastructure migration that closed several security gaps, removed direct database access, moved services behind CloudFront, and eliminated our dependency on DevOps for standard deployments — work that required auditing our entire architecture for unused EC2 instances, orphaned Lambdas, and missing VPC attachments.
	•	I was also part of the team that first integrated our Unified Observability Platform (UVP), building the initial error-tracing setup across both UI and API layers with no established precedent to follow internally.
	•	
These projects taught me something I could not have learned from coursework alone: that good software engineering is inseparable from good judgment under ambiguity — knowing when a model needs retraining versus a different architecture, when automation saves more time than it costs to build, and when a migration is worth the risk of touching a live system.
This is precisely why I am pursuing a Master's in Computer Science now. My electronics foundation gave me systems thinking; my professional experience gave me the ability to ship and scale software; what I am missing is the formal depth in algorithms, distributed systems, and applied AI that would let me design these systems rather than only build and maintain them. I want to move from being the engineer who integrates a model into a pipeline to the engineer who can design the pipeline itself — with the theoretical grounding to make it robust, scalable, and defensible under scrutiny.
I am specifically drawn to Deggendorf's Applied Computer Science program because of how directly it maps onto the gap I am trying to close. The program is built for engineers coming from Applied Computer Science, Infotronics, and related technical degrees to formalize practical knowledge with theoretical and mathematical depth — which is precisely my position, coming from an Electronics and Communication Engineering background with two years of hands-on software engineering experience.
